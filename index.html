SIADH Algorithm Furst-guided

Stepwise diagnostic and management algorithms for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis, with the Furst U/P electrolyte ratio to predict fluid-restriction response and set the restriction target. Updated with contemporary 2025–2026 evidence, including the NEJM 2023 review.

Units
Electrolytes in mmol/L · osmolality in mOsm/kg
Diagnostic algorithm
Sequential gates based on the Bartter–Schwartz criteria. Each gate must be passed before proceeding.
Management algorithm
Severity triage → Furst ratio → fluid restriction target → 24–48 h reassessment → escalation if needed.
Second-line therapy — when fluid restriction fails
Indicated when Furst ratio > 1.0, urine osmolality > 500 mOsm/kg, urine specific gravity > 1.020, urine Na > 130 mmol/L, or serum Na rises ≤ 3 mmol/L over 48 h on < 1 L/day restriction (Tzoulis definition).
⚠️ Isotonic (0.9%) saline is not indicated in SIAD
In SIAD, the sodium in isotonic saline is excreted while free water is retained — isotonic saline worsens hyponatremia. Reserve IV fluids for hypovolemic hyponatremia or use 3% hypertonic saline for severe symptomatic SIAD.

⚖️ Urea vs Empagliflozin — Evidence Comparison
⚠️ No head-to-head trial exists. The two agents' efficacy figures are not directly comparable: the urea meta-analysis pooled heterogeneous observational cohorts including many severely hyponatremic inpatients (where Na rises are inherently larger), whereas the empagliflozin crossover trial enrolled relatively mild, stable outpatients (median baseline Na 131 mmol/L).

Practical decision framework
  • Favor empagliflozin when cardiorenal comorbidity (HF, CKD, diabetic/hypertensive nephropathy) or palatability concerns dominate — it has the stronger (randomized) evidence base and added cardiorenal benefits.
  • Favor oral urea when cost or drug access is the constraint — it is cheaper, widely available, and has a longer clinical track record, albeit observational only.
  • Either agent: monitor serum Na closely during initiation — both work by osmotic diuresis and both can overcorrect.
  • Larger comparative studies are needed to define relative placement. The NEJM review frames empagliflozin as an emerging option whose effect "warrants further study."

⚠️ Severe symptomatic hyponatremia
Seizures, coma, or severe neurologic symptoms → 3% hypertonic saline regardless of the Furst ratio. Correct by 4–6 mmol/L in the first hour if severe, then slow; maximum 8–10 mmol/L per 24 h to avoid osmotic demyelination. Consider a desmopressin clamp (1–2 µg IV/SC every 6–8 h) if overcorrection occurs or is anticipated.
📊 Garrahy et al. RCT (JCEM 2020) — what "modest rise" means
In 46 outpatients with chronic SIAD, fluid restriction to 1 L/day raised plasma sodium by a median of 3 mmol/L at day 4 (vs 1 mmol/L with no treatment), and 4 mmol/L at day 30 (vs 1 mmol/L). Only 17% of restricted patients achieved a rise ≥ 5 mmol/L by day 4. More than a third failed to reach ≥ 130 mmol/L — underscoring the need for second-line options in the ~50% who fail.
Practical points
  • Fluid restriction remains first-line for chronic, non-severely symptomatic SIAD — but it fails in roughly half of patients and adherence is difficult.
  • Alternative restriction target: ~500 mL/day below the 24-h urine output.
  • Isotonic (0.9%) saline worsens hyponatremia in SIAD — the sodium is excreted while free water is retained.
  • Always treat the underlying cause and stop offending drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, PPIs, NSAIDs, MDMA).
  • Monitor serum Na every 2–4 h during active correction, then daily once stable.
